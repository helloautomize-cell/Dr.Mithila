### Intro (answer-first)

A psychological assessment uses conversation, history and standardised tests to answer a specific question about how a person thinks, learns, feels or behaves. At The Mental Health in Borivali West, clinical psychologist Dr. Mithila Desai carries out assessments for children, teenagers and adults, and explains the results and next steps in plain words.

### At a glance

- **Common questions:** attention and learning, intellectual ability, emotional difficulties, personality, career aptitude
- **What it includes:** interview, history, questionnaires and tests, and a feedback session
- **Time:** usually more than one sitting `[CONFIRM: typical number and length]`
- **Report:** a written report `[CONFIRM: typical time to receive it]`
- **Certificates:** official disability certificates are issued by government medical boards

### Types of assessment

- **Intellectual ability (IQ) testing:** how a person reasons, solves problems and learns
- **Learning and attention:** reading, writing, maths, focus and memory (see [ADHD, learning and neurodiversity](/psychological-assessment/adhd-learning-neurodiversity/))
- **Emotional and behavioural:** anxiety, mood, behaviour and their impact
- **Personality:** patterns in how a person thinks, feels and relates to others
- **Aptitude and interests:** strengths and suitable study or career paths (see [Career guidance](/career-guidance/))

### What happens

1. **First meeting.** Dr. Desai understands the question you want answered and gathers history. For children, she meets the parents.
2. **Testing.** Questionnaires and tasks, chosen for the question. Children often find these like puzzles and games.
3. **Other information.** School reports or teacher forms, with your consent.
4. **Feedback.** A session to go through the findings, answer questions and plan support.
5. **Report.** A written summary with practical recommendations.

Dr. Desai chooses standardised assessments based on the question you need answered, and explains the results in plain words. Her reports help families, schools and individuals plan support, and can support applications to government medical boards for certificates.

### How to prepare

- A good night's sleep and a meal beforehand
- Glasses or hearing aids, if used
- Previous reports, school report cards and notebooks for children
- For children: tell them they will do puzzles and activities, not an exam

### FAQs

<div class="faq">
<details><summary>Is an assessment the same as a diagnosis?<i></i></summary><p>An assessment gathers information to answer a question. Sometimes the findings match a recognised condition, such as a specific learning disorder; sometimes they show strengths and areas to support without a label. Dr. Desai will explain clearly what the results mean.</p></details>
<details><summary>Can my child "fail" the test?<i></i></summary><p>No. There is no pass or fail. The aim is to understand how your child learns and what help would suit them. Children do well when they are relaxed and know it is not an exam.</p></details>
<details><summary>Will the school accept the report?<i></i></summary><p>Many schools use a psychologist's report to plan support in class. For board exam accommodations, boards usually require a certificate from a government hospital or medical board; Dr. Desai can explain the process.</p></details>
<details><summary>Can adults be assessed?<i></i></summary><p>Yes. Adults come for questions about attention, learning, personality, or career changes, and to understand long-standing difficulties.</p></details>
