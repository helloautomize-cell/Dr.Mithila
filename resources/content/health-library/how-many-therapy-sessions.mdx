## First, what does "working" mean?

Before counting sessions, it helps to define what better looks like for you. For one person it is fewer panic episodes. For another, getting through the workday without tears, or arguing less at home. Therapy is not an antibiotic with a fixed course: change tends to come in stages. First you understand the pattern, then you learn skills, then you practise until they start to hold.

Many people notice small shifts within a few sessions: clearer thinking, feeling lighter after talking, catching an old reaction early. The deeper and older the difficulty, the longer it takes for change to feel stable.

These are broad shapes used to plan, never guarantees. What matters more than any number is that your psychologist reviews the plan with you openly after the first few appointments, and adjusts it as you go.

## What shapes the number

Severity and duration lead the list: something building for ten years usually takes longer than a rough month at work. Goals matter too: coping better with stress is a different scope from changing deep relationship patterns. Support around you, and how much you practise between sessions, also move the pace. A good psychologist is transparent about all of this and invites questions.

## How often you meet matters as much

Weekly sessions are the usual starting rhythm: close enough to keep momentum, spaced enough to try new skills in between. Once things stabilise, many people move to fortnightly, then occasional check-ins. Some mix online and in-person sessions to keep the rhythm sustainable around commutes and work.

<Warn label="Worth knowing:" text="be cautious if a therapist pushes very frequent sessions without a clear reason, or avoids discussing fees and your constraints. Equally, if you keep cancelling or spreading sessions too thin, progress will naturally slow. It is better to plan honestly for what you can sustain." />

## How to tell therapy is helping

Track concrete changes rather than a session count. You react slightly differently to familiar stress. You can name feelings more precisely than "fine" or "messed up". Bad days pass faster. Old patterns still appear, but you catch them in the moment. People around you notice you are calmer or clearer. Your psychologist may also use simple scales or check-in questions to make these shifts visible.

## Getting the most from each one

Arrive with a rough sense of what felt hardest that week. Be honest, even when it is embarrassing. Practise at least one thing between sessions. Keep brief notes. And say so if something is not landing: therapy is a collaboration, not a lecture.

## When to discuss extending or ending

If several weeks pass with no movement, raise it in session. The plan can be adjusted, or occasionally a different fit helps. And when you feel much better, you do not need to keep coming "just in case". Many people switch to occasional check-ins, treating therapy as maintenance rather than crisis care.
