## What an EAP actually covers

An employee assistance programme gives structure to workplace mental health support, but the term is often sold as a black box. A real EAP is more than a helpline and a poster.

A well-designed programme covers confidential one-to-one counselling (online or in person), guidance for managers and HR on responding to distress, workshops on stress and boundaries, support for specific concerns like grief or relationship strain, and a clear referral path when someone needs longer-term therapy. When you evaluate a provider, ask them to walk you through what happens from the moment an employee reaches out, step by step. If that flow is vague, the programme will be hard to trust.

## What employees look for

The programmes people actually use share three things: easy access, real confidentiality, and visible support from leadership. Employees do not want to email HR to get a counselling slot; they want a direct route to a qualified professional. Flexible online sessions matter for busy staff, and coverage hours should match how your workforce actually works — a 9-to-6 line helps no one on shifts.

## The counselling itself

Workplace counselling should be provided by trained mental health professionals who understand organisational life: deadlines, performance pressure, team politics, role ambiguity. Employees typically bring anxiety, low mood, relationship strain, family conflict and grief. The counsellor's job is not to make the employee productive again for the company's sake; it is to support them properly, with clinical ethics intact.

A good model uses the EAP as a first step, then refers people who need deeper work into structured individual therapy outside the programme, with informed consent and clear boundaries about what, if anything, HR is told.

## Beyond one-off talks

A single annual workshop changes little. What works is a rhythm: regular group sessions, open office hours with a psychologist, and steady communication that keeps the door visible. Quieter employees may never speak in a group session, but they remember the name, and reach out later.

## Measuring impact without breaking privacy

Employers legitimately want to know the programme is used. The safe way is aggregated, de-identified reporting agreed upfront: session counts, broad concern categories, general themes. Patterns like rising work-stress calls in one function are useful signal. Names, performance links and case details are not.

## Choosing a provider

Ask who actually does the counselling and what their training is. Ask how they respond to high-risk situations, for example an employee expressing thoughts of self-harm. Request a sample anonymised usage report and communication plan. Providers who think like clinicians answer these easily; generic vendors struggle.
