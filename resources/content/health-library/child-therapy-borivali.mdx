## When a phase becomes a pattern

Children have phases. A few weeks of tantrums after a big change is ordinary. The sign worth acting on is a pattern that lasts for more than a month or two and starts affecting school, sleep, friendships or health.

## What a first session looks like

Most parents worry the psychologist will blame their parenting or label the child quickly. In reality, the first meeting is mostly about understanding and building comfort.

## How child therapy works week to week

From the outside it can look like play. Underneath, the therapist is working on naming emotions, handling disappointment, problem-solving and expressing needs, using games, stories, role play or simple exercises matched to the child's age.

Parents are always part of it: brief check-ins, separate parent meetings, or things to practise at home. Without that, change in the therapy room rarely survives the week.

## Working with school

Emotional progress at home can still wobble at exam time if school is not on board. A psychologist can guide you on how to talk to teachers, ask for reasonable adjustments, and decide how much to share. With your permission they can send a short note summarising strategies that help your child, without disclosing more than you choose.

## A word on teenagers

Teens carry exam pressure, social media comparison and family expectations all at once, and what looks like a discipline problem is often burnout, anxiety or low mood. Teen sessions start with a private, non-judgemental space where confidentiality's limits are explained clearly. Parents join at agreed points to work on rules and communication, so home becomes calmer too.
