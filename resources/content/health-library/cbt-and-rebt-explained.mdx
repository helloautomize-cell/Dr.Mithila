## Two therapies, one shared idea

CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy) and REBT (rational emotive behaviour therapy) are cousins. Both work on the idea that your thoughts affect your feelings and actions, and both are well studied for anxiety.

In CBT, the focus is on identifying unhelpful thoughts, "I will definitely mess up this presentation", and testing them against evidence. In REBT, the focus is on the deeper beliefs underneath, "I must never fail; if I do, I am worthless", and learning to replace them with more flexible, realistic ones.

A simple way to picture it: CBT examines whether this particular thought stands up in court. REBT questions the strict inner rules that keep producing such thoughts in the first place.

## How CBT works for anxiety

Thoughts, feelings and behaviours form a loop, and anxiety often lives in how that loop gets stuck. A CBT plan for anxiety usually involves noticing patterns like catastrophising, mind reading or all-or-nothing thinking, then testing those thoughts through structured exercises and small behavioural experiments.

Someone anxious about travelling might, for example, list what has actually happened on past journeys, rate their anxiety before and after trips, and slowly expand what they are willing to do rather than avoiding travel altogether.

CBT is typically structured: clear goals, worksheets, and small tasks between sessions. Many people find that clarity practical.

## How REBT works for anxiety

REBT, developed by psychologist Albert Ellis, says it is not events themselves that upset us but the rigid beliefs we hold about them. For anxiety these often sound like "I must always be in control" or "People must approve of me."

REBT work involves spotting three kinds of unhelpful beliefs: demands ("must" and "should"), low frustration tolerance ("I cannot bear this"), and global ratings ("I am useless if I fail"). You then actively dispute them and practise more flexible alternatives. "I must never make a mistake at work" becomes "I strongly prefer to do well, but a mistake is human and does not define me." Sessions can feel quite direct: describe a recent upset, find what you told yourself, and challenge it, usually with a small written task to take away.

## How to decide for yourself

Rather than getting stuck on labels, think about what you want from therapy: practical strategies for a specific trigger, deeper work on perfectionism, or a bit of both. Then ask the psychologist directly: "How do you usually work with anxiety? Do you use CBT, REBT, or a mix? What would the first month look like for someone with my concerns?" Their answers will tell you more than any article, including this one.

## When self-help stops being enough

Books, videos and breathing exercises help, but there are clear signs to speak to a professional: anxiety affecting sleep, work or relationships, or starting to avoid ordinary things like commuting or meeting friends.
